The morning ransomware took 1,500 servers down

A blameless postmortem. In 2022, ransomware took 1,500+ servers and 80 to 90 services per environment offline at Zycus, across 7 environments. We had the fleet back in 7 days and spent the next 4 months hardening it. I was one of the first responders.

Details that could help an attacker, like how it got in, are left out on purpose. What's here is the order things happened in, the decisions, and what changed.

Day one

  1. 07:00

    I get a call: something is down. The first report is vague, and it gets the priority vague reports usually get. Nobody treats it as an incident yet.

  2. Morning

    The main data server goes down. I trace the outage to it, and at first I read it as ordinary slowness.

  3. About 10:00

    We confirm it: this is ransomware, and it is spreading.

  4. Next

    I go to the office, pull the affected server and shut everything down to stop the blast radius. Every service is now offline, on purpose. It is the most expensive decision of the day and the one that made recovery possible.

  5. Same day

    I build a single standalone machine, cut off from the network, so we can reach data and VMware without touching anything that might still be infected.

Days 1 to 7: rebuild in pieces

You can't turn a compromised network back on and hope. We disconnected the infrastructure into pieces, isolated and checked each one, and brought back only what was clean.

  • Isolate, check, restore. One piece at a time, so a missed infection couldn't reach everything again.
  • Zscaler, immediately. Rolled out as part of the response, not as a later project.
  • Backups and new servers. Restored from backup and rebuilt servers rather than cleaning the old ones.
  • Forensics. I worked with the forensic team to understand how it got in, so the rebuild closed that path.

By day 7, the fleet was back across all 7 environments.

The next 4 months: hardening

Restoring service was the urgent part. The bigger job was making sure the next one couldn't spread the same way, from the cloud network down to the OS on every server.

  • Network: re-architected around AWS Transit Gateway and segmented VPCs, with 20 Palo Alto firewalls between segments.
  • Endpoints and access: CrowdStrike on servers and in CI/CD, Zscaler for access, CyberArk for privileged accounts.
  • Recovery: Veeam for backup and disaster recovery.
  • OS: fleet upgrades, including CentOS 7.4 to 7.9.
  • Operations: restructured how the team ran day to day after the incident.

What I took from it

The first signal is usually vague

About three hours passed between the first call and a confirmed attack. The person who called wasn't the problem. The process was: a vague report had no path to someone who would treat it as a possible incident. Now I want a short checklist that turns "something is down" into a severity call within minutes.

A flat network has one stop button

Shutting everything down was right, but it was right because there was nothing smaller to shut down. Segmentation means next time you isolate a segment, not the company.

Build the clean room before you need it

The standalone recovery machine was built under pressure on day one. It should exist in advance, offline, with a runbook taped to it.

Backups count only if the attack can't reach them

Recovery speed came down to having something clean to restore from. Backups that live on the same network as production are just more targets.
