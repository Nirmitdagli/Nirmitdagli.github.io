Quantum computing research · Quinnipiac University

When does a quantum computer actually beat a classical one?

My M.S. thesis measured it, instead of assuming it. HERO runs the same workloads on CPU, GPU and QPU tiers and compares accuracy, latency, energy and cloud cost across 30 seeds each.

I come from infrastructure, so I treat a QPU the way I treat a GPU: one more tier a scheduler can place work on, if the numbers say it should.

What the thesis found

Two workloads, two opposite answers.

Cybersecurity · KDD Cup 99 intrusion detection

~10,000x

less energy on classical hardware. Random Forest reached F1 0.998; the quantum SVM reached 0.667.

On cost, a classical SVM comes to about $1.99 per million classifications. The quantum SVM comes to about $2.1 million per million at 2026 cloud prices. For this problem, today, classical wins on every axis.

Molecular simulation · H₂ ground state via VQE

n > 50

qubits: the point where classical simulation hits its memory wall and quantum takes over.

For H₂ itself, exact diagonalization used 0.04 J against 29,833 J for VQE, so classical still wins at small sizes. But classical memory grows exponentially with system size, and past about 50 qubits it can't keep up. That is where a QPU earns its place.

Why this matters
Most quantum pitches skip the part where classical wins. A scheduler that knows both answers can route each job to the tier that is actually cheaper, faster or greener, and that is the useful version of hybrid computing.
How it was measured
QSVM, QAOA, Grover and VQE on Qiskit Aer, against SVM, Random Forest, gradient boosting and KNN baselines. 30 seeds per experiment, with cloud costs from AWS, IBM and Azure pricing.

The scheduler

Workload-aware tier allocation

HERO picks a starting tier from the workload type and size, measures every tier, keeps the Pareto-optimal options on cost, energy, latency and accuracy, then picks one using the user's weights.

HERO-S extends it to AI workloads across CPU, GPU, cloud and edge, using an 8×H100 cost model. Against cloud-only placement it cut energy by 72 to 98%.

HERO-S: QPU-Aware Energy-Latency Orchestration for AIoT Intrusion Detection

The question. Intrusion detection for AI-enabled IoT now spans edge gateways, cloud AI accelerators and optional remote quantum services. For each security task, should it stay at the edge, escalate to cloud inference, or take a QPU-capable path, given latency, queue time and energy limits?

The approach. HERO-S tags every task with alert severity, asset criticality, uncertainty, response deadline, backend queue time, route confidence and an energy estimate, then picks a route with a constrained utility. Evaluated on TON_IoT, NSL-KDD, CICIDS2017 and IoT-23 over 30 seeds.

  • · The cloud model is the quality ceiling, but it breaks a 2.5 ms per-sample gateway budget.
  • · Inside that budget, HERO-S beats edge-only and a classical uncertainty-cascade baseline on F1 on the three non-trivial datasets, and stays near-perfect at the edge on IoT-23.
  • · At 250 ms QPU queue time it sends every sample to edge or cloud: the scheduler explicitly decides when a remote QPU isn't worth the wait.

SEET-26 · accepted

0.998 vs 0.667

F1, Random Forest vs quantum SVM

HERO: Cost-Energy-Latency Trade-offs in Hybrid Quantum-Classical Cloud Workloads

The question. Clouds now rent CPU instances, NVIDIA H100 GPUs and QPU services (IBM Quantum, Amazon Braket) by the second. Given a workload, which tier gives the best mix of cost, energy and latency without losing accuracy?

The approach. A measurement framework run on two opposite case studies, each repeated 30 times against four classical baselines (SVM, Random Forest, gradient boosting, KNN).

  • · Intrusion detection (KDD Cup 99): the quantum SVM used about 10,000x more energy and cost about $2.1M per million classifications, against $1.99 for a classical SVM.
  • · Molecular simulation (H₂ via VQE): VQE reached chemical accuracy (0.007 Hartree error), but at 854,000x the energy of exact diagonalization at this size.
  • · From the measurements: a workload-aware tier allocation rule, and projected crossover points where quantum becomes the only feasible path.

Quantum Computing: Grover's Algorithm for String Search and Its Practical Limits

Grover's algorithm promises a quadratic speedup for unstructured search: about √N queries instead of N. This paper applies it to string search and looks at the practical limits that decide whether that speedup survives on real hardware.

M.S. thesis · Quinnipiac · May 2026

~50 qubits

where classical simulation runs out

Hybrid Quantum-AI Models for Cybersecurity on Cloud Platforms: Accuracy, Latency, and Energy

The full version of HERO. The central question: how should a workload be split across CPU, GPU and QPU tiers to optimize cost, energy and latency without sacrificing accuracy? Two opposite verdicts: classical wins intrusion detection today, while classical state-vector simulation of molecules scales as O(2ⁿ) and becomes infeasible beyond about 50 qubits, where VQE scales polynomially.

Includes Algorithm 1 (tier allocation), a Pareto-frontier selection method over cost, energy, latency and accuracy, and the projected feature dimension where quantum kernels become cost-competitive. Advisor: Prof. Taskin.
